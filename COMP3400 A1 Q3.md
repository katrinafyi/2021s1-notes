$$
\begin{align*}
\textrm{True} &= \lambda x . (\lambda y . x) &&& \textrm{False} &= \lambda x . (\lambda y . y) \\
\textrm{AND} &= \lambda p (\lambda q . (pq) p) &&& \textrm{OR} &= \lambda p . (\lambda q . (pp) q)
\end{align*}
$$
We need to implement $\operatorname*{XOR}$. 

Firstly, we could try defining it in terms of elementary binary operations (in two ways),
$$
x \oplus y = (x \wedge \neg y) \vee (\neg x \wedge y) = (x \vee y) \wedge \neg (x \wedge y).
$$
However, both of these look like they will be very difficult to reduce, needing 3 binary operations as well as some sort of negation. We can reason with the functions to save a bit of work.

Looking at the definitions, we can think of true and false as binary functions of two arguments (which we will call left and right). Then, true is like a "select left" function and false is like "select right". 

Let's build a negation function $\operatorname*{NOT}$ because we forsee it will be useful. With the interpretation of true and false as above, it is easy to write
$$
\operatorname*{NOT} = \lambda p.p\, \operatorname*{False}\operatorname*{True}.
$$
If $p$ is true, false will be selected and returned, otherwise true will be returned. This is nice but unfortunately problematic. Expanding the definitions, we find that we cannot reduce it
$$
\lambda p.p\, \operatorname*{False}\operatorname*{True} = \lambda p.p\,(\lambda x.\lambda y.y)\,(\lambda x.\lambda y.x).
$$
It would be nice to have a simpler form for not which we can work with easier. Returning to the original definitions, we can see that *flipping* the arguments to true or false will effectively negate the boolean. That is,
$$
\operatorname*{NOT} = \lambda p.\lambda x.\lambda y.p\,y\,x \implies \operatorname*{NOT} p = \lambda x.\lambda y.p\,y\,x.
$$
It is easy to check that this fulfills the definitions.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{NOT} \operatorname*{True}&= \lambda x.\lambda y.\operatorname*{True} y\, x \\
&= \lambda x.\lambda y.(\lambda x.\lambda y. x)\,y\,x \\ 
&\to \lambda x.\lambda y. (\lambda x'.\lambda y'. x')\,y\,x \\ 
&\to\lambda x.\lambda y.(\lambda y'.x')[x':=y]\,x \\ 
&=\lambda x.\lambda y.(\lambda y'.y) \,x \\ 
&\to \lambda x.\lambda y.(y)[y':=x] \\ 
&=\lambda x.\lambda y.y \\ 
&= \operatorname*{False}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{NOT} \operatorname*{False}&= \lambda x.\lambda y.\operatorname*{False} y\, x \\
&= \lambda x.\lambda y.(\lambda x.\lambda y. y)\,y\,x \\ 
&\to \lambda x.\lambda y. (\lambda x'.\lambda y'. y')\,y\,x \\ 
&\to\lambda x.\lambda y.(\lambda y'.y')[x':=y]\,x \\ 
&=\lambda x.\lambda y.(\lambda y'.y') \,x \\ 
&\to \lambda x.\lambda y.(y')[y':=x] \\ 
&=\lambda x.\lambda y.x \\ 
&= \operatorname*{True}
\end{aligned}
$$

With our new negation superpowers, how do we implement XOR? Keep in mind the selection interpretation of true/false and consider $x \oplus y$. If $x$ is true, then the result is $\neg y$. If $x$ is false, the result is $y$. We can actually directly write this as
$$
\operatorname*{XOR} = \lambda p.\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q
$$
which works just like that. Expanding and reducing, we get
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{XOR} &=\lambda p.\lambda q.p\, ((\lambda p.\lambda x.\lambda y.p\,y\,x) \,q)\,q \\ 
&\to\lambda p.\lambda q.p\, ((\lambda p'.\lambda x'.\lambda y'.p'\,y'\,x') \,q)\,q && \text{alpha conversion} \\ 
&\to\lambda p.\lambda q.p\, ((\lambda x'.\lambda y'.p'\,y'\,x')[p':=q])\,q &&\text{beta reduction} \\ 
&=\lambda p.\lambda q.p\, (\lambda x'.\lambda y'.q\,y'\,x')\,q &&\text{bind}
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the beta normal form and we are (finally) done.

We can quickly verify that this does indeed perform XOR. We omit the working for reducing $\operatorname*{NOT}$ because 
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{XOR} \operatorname*{False} \operatorname*{False} 
&= (\lambda p.\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{False} \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to(\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) [p:=\operatorname*{False}] \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&=(\lambda q. \operatorname*{False} \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to (\lambda q. q) \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to \operatorname*{False}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{XOR} \operatorname*{False} \operatorname*{True} 
&= (\lambda p.\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{False} \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to(\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) [p:=\operatorname*{False}] \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&=(\lambda q. \operatorname*{False} \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to (\lambda q. q) \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to \operatorname*{True}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{XOR} \operatorname*{True} \operatorname*{False} 
&= (\lambda p.\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{True} \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to(\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) [p:=\operatorname*{True}] \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&=(\lambda q. \operatorname*{True} \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to (\lambda q. \operatorname*{NOT}q) \operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to \operatorname*{NOT}\operatorname*{False} \\ 
&\to \operatorname*{True}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{XOR} \operatorname*{True} \operatorname*{True} 
&= (\lambda p.\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{True} \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to(\lambda q. p \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) [p:=\operatorname*{True}] \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&=(\lambda q. \operatorname*{True} \,(\operatorname*{NOT} q)\,q) \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to (\lambda q. \operatorname*{NOT}q) \operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to \operatorname*{NOT}\operatorname*{True} \\ 
&\to \operatorname*{False}
\end{aligned}
$$

-----------

Alternatively, this can be written as somewhat more clearly as
$$
\operatorname*{XOR}' = \lambda p.\lambda q.\lambda x.\lambda y. p \,(q\,y\,x)\,(q\,x\,y)
$$
which is intuitively the same. Interestingly, $\operatorname*{XOR}$ and $\operatorname*{XOR}'$ cannot beta reduce to one another.
